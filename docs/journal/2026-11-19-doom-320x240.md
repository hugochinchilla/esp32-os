# Doom at 320x240: letting the monitor make the pixels large

2026-11-19, the evening Doom first ran on the board. Backlog item 81.
Follows [Doom](2026-11-19-doom.md). The owner asked for this entry for the
video ("añade una memoria de esto para el video de youtube").

## 1. What was asked

The first board turn had Doom running at 26 to 35 pictures a second. The
owner:

> 26-35 fps? parece que teniendo acceso directo a la pantalla debería ser
> capaz de obtener más.
>
> Probemos a desconectar la wifi mientras se juega, crees que eso puede
> ayudar? o tienes tus propias hipotesis de por que no va a 60fps?

The answer he got was four guesses, none of them the Wi-Fi: the engine of
1993 moves the game 35 times a second and draws one picture for each, so
35 is its ceiling; of the 20 to 26 ms a picture took, 11.6 ms went to
putting it on the screen; everything the game touches is in the external
RAM, which the video output reads all the time as well; and a picture that
takes more than 1/35 s loses a turn. He read "11.6 ms to put it on the
screen" and saw at once what the agent had not proposed:

> un momento, si nos lleva tanto tiempo doblar la pantalla, dejemos que el
> monitor haga ese trabajo, no podemos hacer una señal vga de 320x240?

And, on being told how: "que bueno, hagamos el truco".

## 2. What was found

Doom draws 320 by 200 pixels. The screen is 640 by 480, so the program made
each of Doom's pixels into four: 256,000 bytes written to the picture for
every one of the game's, through four tables that also dither the colour
(the board shows 8 levels of red and green and 4 of blue). The picture is
in the external RAM (PSRAM), which is slow to write, and the video
interrupt reads the whole of it 59.52 times a second: 307,200 bytes a
frame, 18 MB a second, on the same bus as the game's code and data.

**A monitor will not take a signal of 320x240.** A VGA monitor wants its
lines at about 31.5 kHz, and 240 lines sixty times a second are half that.
The lowest thing it shows is the 525 lines of 640x480.

**But it cannot count pixels.** A VGA signal has no pixel clock in it: the
monitor sees a sync pulse at the start of each line and a voltage that
changes along it. If the board changes the voltage half as often, the
monitor sees the same line with pixels twice as wide. So across, the
doubling is free: halve the pixel clock, from 25 MHz to 12.5 MHz, and send
320 pixels in the same 25.6 us.

**Down, each line has to go twice,** since the monitor wants its 480. The
video driver already copies the picture line by line into two small
buffers of internal RAM as the signal goes out (ten lines at a time, every
320 us; `on_bounce_empty` in `firmware/os/main/vga.c`). Copying each line
twice there costs less than what it did before: it reads half as much from
the external RAM, 1,600 bytes instead of 6,400, and makes the second copy
from the first, inside the fast memory.

This is not a new trick. It is what the VGA card of 1987 did with mode
13h, the 320x200 in 256 colours that Doom was written for: the card sent a
400-line signal with a halved dot clock and every line scanned twice. The
monitors of 1993 never saw a 320x200 signal either. The board now does
what the card did.

## 3. The options

Put to the owner on a card:

1. **320x240 for real**, as above. The program writes 64,000 bytes a
   picture instead of 256,000 and the video reads a quarter of what it
   read. The price: the dither. With four screen pixels for each of Doom's,
   the four could take four different levels and the eye mixed them; with
   one for one, the mixing has to happen over four of Doom's own pixels,
   a grain twice as coarse, and dark walls may show more bands.
2. **640x240**: the driver repeats lines, the program still doubles
   across. Half the cost of now, the dither stays fine across, and the
   signal does not change at all.
3. **Wait** for the board's figures (a measuring turn was already asked
   for) before touching the video.

He chose the first. The agent had recommended it: it saves the most and it
is how the game was shown in its day; and going to the second later, if
the bands bother, is a small change.

## 4. What was built

- `firmware/os/main/vga.c`: a third mode, 320x240 at a 12.5 MHz pixel
  clock, 400 by 525 in all, 59.52 pictures a second, the syncs of 640x480.
  A mode now says how many times each of its lines is sent; the panel is
  told 480 lines and the refill makes them from 240. The pointer is not
  mixed in. The mode is not on the list of modes a screen can be set to
  (`VGA_MODE_COARSE`).
- `core/include/core/ports.hpp`: `Display::set_coarse(on)`. A screen that
  has no such picture says no; the board's says yes, the PC's test screen
  when a test asks.
- `core/src/wm/wm.cpp`: `lend_screen(window, coarse)`. The desktop keeps
  its own size while the screen is coarse, and is painted again when the
  loan ends; the remote screen is told at both ends.
- `os::ui::take_screen(window, out, coarse)`: the program asks, and reads
  in `out` what it got.
- `programs/doom/doom.cpp`: asks for the coarse picture. With it, a pixel
  for a pixel, 320x200 in the middle of 320x240 (20 black lines above and
  below, 40 on the monitor), the four dither tables laid over squares of
  two by two. Without it (the PC's windows), as before.

The decision is `docs/decisions/coarse-screen.md`.

## 5. How it was tested

On the PC: `test_os` (asking where the screen has none gives the screen as
it was; where it has, 320x240, black, the desktop's size untouched, the
remote screen's listener told twice, the desktop painted again afterwards)
and `test_doom` (the title picture at 320x240 with the shareware WAD,
black above and below, the logo key back to a painted desktop of 640x480).
The firmware compiles and links in the cloud (1,699,376 bytes; `doom.app`
355,008 bytes).

Not tested: the signal. Nothing of this has run on the board, and no
monitor has been shown a 12.5 MHz picture from it. What can go wrong
there, and is asked of the board turn: whether the monitor keeps the
picture through the change or looks for the signal again, whether the
picture is as wide as before, and whether the LCD peripheral's divider
gives 12.5 MHz as cleanly as it gives 25.

Not measured: how long a picture takes now. The estimate given to the
owner, 2 to 3 ms in place of 11.6, is a guess from the bytes.

## 6. What is left

- The board turn: the picture, the figures of the log (pictures a second,
  the round split into screen, waiting and game), missed refills.
- The other suspect for the drops from 35 to 26, which this does not
  touch: when the engine is ahead of its clock it waits with `I_Sleep(1)`,
  and the board's FreeRTOS counts time in steps of 10 ms.
- 60 pictures a second needs pictures drawn between two moves of the game
  (as Crispy Doom does): a change to the engine, not asked for yet.
- The Linux program and the web page do not offer the coarse picture; Doom
  there doubles its pixels as before.

## 7. On the board, the same evening

Two board turns, one before the change (commit `d573406`) and one after
(`6c786c4`), with the log line that splits a round into its parts.

| | pictures a second | a round | to the screen | waiting | the game |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 640x480, the program doubles, Wi-Fi on | 34.3 to 35.1 | 20 to 22 ms | 11.6 ms | about 0 | 8 to 10 ms |
| the same, Wi-Fi off | the same | | | | |
| the same, a remote viewer connected | 29.8 to 31.0 | | 13.3 ms | | 13 ms |
| 320x240, Wi-Fi on or off | 35.1, steady | 11 to 12 ms | 3.15 ms | 1.4 ms | 6 to 7 ms |

So: the Wi-Fi costs nothing that can be measured. The waiting was not the
thief the agent suspected (about 0 before; 1.4 ms now, because the round
ends sooner and the engine has longer to wait for its clock). Putting the
picture on the screen went from 11.6 ms to 3.15 ms, close to the guess of
2 to 3, and the game itself got faster as well, 8 to 10 ms down to 6 to 7,
with the video reading a quarter of what it read from the external RAM. A
round is now about a third of the 28.5 ms there are.

The signal: the board's log says `screen: 320x240, pixel clock 12500000
Hz`, a frame every 16,800 us as at 640x480. Seen through a webcam, the
monitor held the picture through the change and the game fills the width;
the dither shows. The owner had not yet said what he thinks of the
colours. No refill was missed. Every way out (the logo key, Ctrl+Alt+Del,
the game's menu, End Task) brought back 640x480 and the memory, and from
800x600 the screen went back to 800x600. A remote viewer is dropped at
both changes and connects again, as decided.

One thing to look into, not confirmed: after the first session of Doom the
largest free block of internal RAM was 36,864 bytes where it had been
59,392, and did not come back.

At 11 to 12 ms a round there is room for a picture every 16.8 ms. The
engine still makes 35: 60 needs it to draw between two moves of the game.

## 8. And 60?

Asked on a card whether to go for 60 now, later on a branch of its own, or
not at all, the owner chose to stay at 35: Doom as it was. The engine is
left as doomgeneric has it.
